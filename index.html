A technical review of KERS and modern hybrid power units in contemporary Formula 1.

Introduction

The article offers a technical look at the Formula 1 Energy Recovery Systems (ERS) – from the 2009 KERS system to the current hybrid power units. It discusses the engineering concepts of the MGU-K and MGU-H, how they have been combined with turbocharged internal combustion engines, and how they can be made more efficient and optimised. It also looks at thermodynamic cycles and track applications, demonstrating how F1 developments are improving mechanical and electrical engineering and being applied in hybrid road-car technologies.

From KERS to ERS

Today's F1 power units generate around 870 hp through advanced hybridisation, with approximately 160 hp coming from braking energy and exhaust heat (F1 Chronicle, Grix Me). This is a significant improvement over KERS, which was primarily a system that recovered braking energy. ERS is a thermodynamic and electrical engineering approach to enhancing the performance of powertrains and has also had an impact on modern hybrid and electric vehicles.

The two main components of ERS are the Motor Generator Unit-Kinetic (MGU-K) which captures kinetic energy at braking and the Motor Generator Unit-Heat (MGU-H) which captures energy from the exhaust gases and the turbocharger (Flow Racers).

KERS was the first major step of hybridisation in Formula 1 in 2009 and was made compulsory in 2011 (Wikipedia). It was used to store energy for subsequent electrical or mechanical use, and generated approximately 60 kW/80 hp, 400 kJ/lap and 6.67 seconds of maximum usage (Baba Automobile; Mechanical ANITS, N.D.). KERS used either lithium-ion batteries or mechanical flywheels, such as the Ferrari HY-KERS which was approximately 5kg and spun at 64,500 rpm. Even though it was prone to overheating, reliability and weight problems, KERS paved the way towards modern ERS.

Hybrid V6 Turbo Era

In 2014, Formula One adopted the mandatory hybridised power units, moving away from the V8 engine to a 1.6-litre V6 turbocharged hybrid engine that is more efficient and brings Formula One technology into the realm of sustainable road-car development (Wikipedia). The early V6s produced about 710 hp without any hybrid assistance, compared with over 750 hp from the previous V8s, so the hybridisation was important to restore the power as well as improve the efficiency (Wikipedia). ERS also allowed for more energy recovery than KERS, with a maximum power increase of 120 kW (about 160 hp) due to the recovery of exhaust heat (Grix Me) from the MGU-H. The MGU-K was introduced in 2014 and is based on KERS technology.

ERS Components and Operation

Today, FIA regulations control ERS recovery and deployment for safety and competitive balance (FIA). The MGU-K can recover up to two megajoules per lap, while the MGU-H has no recovery limit and typically recovers three to four megajoules. Teams can deploy a maximum of four megajoules per lap, with power limited to 120 kW and operating voltages typically exceeding 600 volts.

ERS consists of four main components: the MGU-K, MGU-H, Energy Store and Power Control Unit. The MGU-K recovers kinetic energy during braking, converting it into electrical energy for storage and later deployment. It can recover up to two megajoules per lap, with 90–95% conversion efficiency, and provide up to 120 kW instantly, making it useful for overtaking and reducing turbo lag.

The MGU-H recovers energy from exhaust gases through the turbocharger and can also operate as an electric motor. It typically recovers three to four megajoules per lap with no recovery limit, providing continuous performance benefits (Flow Racers).

The Energy Store stores recovered energy for later use and can discharge up to four megajoules per lap. Weighing around 25–35 kg and providing approximately 1.1 kWh of capacity, it requires careful thermal management to maintain performance and reliability.

Race Strategy and Thermal Limits

These dynamics are important to consider when planning a race strategy as fuel allowances are restricted to around 100 kg per race. Hybrid systems can be used to allow drivers to increase their speed without burning too much fuel, and they can be used to deploy ERS at higher settings during qualifying to get the most out of each lap. In races, teams need to strategically manage the recovery, storage and deployment of energy as it fluctuates across the laps.

It is also important to deploy energy at the right time. Drivers can save ERS energy over several laps to generate extra power for overtaking or to prevent another car from passing. If ERS energy is not managed properly, they may be unable to apply enough energy when it is required, making deployment timing critical to performance.

Thermal limits are equally important. Overheating of the Energy Store, motor-generator systems or MGU-H can be caused by excessive use, which may lead to a loss of performance and therefore lose valuable overtaking opportunities.

Conclusion

Finally, ERS is the next step in Formula One's evolution to a more efficient hybrid system, which was introduced in 2009 with limited KERS. The MGU-K, which recovers kinetic energy, is integrated with the MGU-H, which recovers thermal energy and provides turbo assistance, to form a very integrated hybrid system. These advances help to improve performance in F1 and are also helping with technologies that are currently being implemented in modern-day consumer cars.
