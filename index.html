Introduction

The article offers a technical look at the Formula 1 Energy Recovery Systems (ERS) – from the 2009 KERS system to the current hybrid power units. It discusses the engineering concepts of the MGU-K and MGU-H, how they have been combined with turbocharged internal combustion engines, and how they can be made more efficient and optimised. It also looks at thermodynamic cycles and track applications, demonstrating how F1 developments are improving mechanical and electrical engineering and being applied in hybrid road-car technologies.

From KERS to ERS

Today's F1 power units deliver around 870 horsepower, a significant improvement over the previous days through hybridisation (F1 Chronicle). Energy from around 160 hp is recovered from braking and exhaust heat (Grix Me). This is a significant improvement over the KERS which would recover energy mainly during braking. Energy Recovery Systems (ERS) are not just a regulatory shift since 2014, but a new approach to the recovery and re-use of wasted energy to boost powertrain performance.

ERS has revolutionized racing and car design. It has two main elements: the Motor Generator Unit-Kinetic (MGU-K) which captures kinetic energy during braking and the Motor Generator Unit-Heat (MGU-H), which will capture energy from the exhaust gases and the turbocharger (Flow Racers). These systems work together to enhance efficiency and performance by combining thermodynamic and electrical engineering. This progress also yields technology applicable to today's hybrid and electric cars.

KERS was the first big step forward in hybridisation for Formula 1 in 2009. It was originally a voluntary component, but was made mandatory in 2011 (Wikipedia). KERS stored kinetic energy when the vehicle was being slowed down and used it to generate electrical or mechanical power to boost acceleration at a later time (Baba Automobile). It generated roughly 60kW/80 hp, 400kJ/lap and around 6.67sec of peak usage. There was some temporary power increase in areas of overtaking and acceleration, but this was not a significant benefit (Mechanical ANITS, N.D.).

KERS was either battery electric or mechanical flywheel. High-voltage lithium-ion batteries, motor generators and control electronics were used in battery systems developed by McLaren/Mercedes, Renault and Ferrari. Ferrari also created the HY-KERS flywheel system that was 240 mm in diameter and weighed approximately 5 kg and rotated at speeds of up to 64,500 rpm (Baba Automobile).

Early KERS systems had problems with overheating, reliability and weight, though. Drivers also needed to adjust their driving techniques to ensure the effective use of energy. In spite of the drawbacks, KERS set the precedent for future ERS, the idea being to recover braking energy for a later use.

Hybrid V6 Turbo Era

In 2014, Formula One adopted the mandatory hybridised power units, moving away from the V8 engine to a 1.6-litre V6 turbocharged hybrid engine that is more efficient and brings Formula One technology into the realm of sustainable road-car development (Wikipedia). The V6s were early engines which produced about 710 hp without any hybridisation, whereas the previous V8s produced more than 750 hp without hybridisation, so the hybridisation was important to restore the power as well as improve the efficiency (Wikipedia). ERS also allowed for more energy recovery than KERS, with a maximum power increase of 120 kW (about 160 hp) due to the recovery of exhaust heat (Grix Me) from the MGU-H. The MGU-K has been around since 2014 and is based on KERS technology.

ERS Components and Operation

Today, FIA regulations control ERS recovery and deployment for safety and competitive balance (FIA). The MGU-K can recover up to two megajoules per lap, while the MGU-H has no recovery limit and typically recovers three to four megajoules. Teams can deploy a maximum of four megajoules per lap, with power limited to 120 kW and operating voltages typically exceeding 600 volts.

ERS consists of four main components: the MGU-K, MGU-H, Energy Store and Power Control Unit. The MGU-K recovers kinetic energy during braking, converting it into electrical energy for storage and later deployment. It can recover up to two megajoules per lap, with 90–95% conversion efficiency, and provide up to 120 kW instantly, making it useful for overtaking and reducing turbo lag.

The MGU-H recovers energy from exhaust gases through the turbocharger and can also operate as an electric motor. It typically recovers three to four megajoules per lap with no recovery limit, providing continuous performance benefits (Flow Racers).

The Energy Store stores recovered energy for later use and can discharge up to four megajoules per lap. Weighing around 25–35 kg and providing approximately 1.1 kWh of capacity, it requires careful thermal management to maintain performance and reliability.

Race Strategy and Thermal Limits

These dynamics are important to consider when planning a race strategy as fuel allowances are restricted to around 100 kg per race. Hybrid systems can be used to allow drivers to increase their speed without burning too much fuel, and they can be used to deploy ERS at higher settings during qualifying to get the most out of each lap. In races, teams need to strategically manage the recovery, storage and deployment of energy as it fluctuates across the laps.

It is also important to deploy the right time. Drivers can store ERS to use for a few laps to generate extra power for overtaking or to prevent another car from passing. If a driver's energy is not managed properly, they may be unable to apply enough energy when it is required, and timing is critical to performance.

Thermal limits are equally important. Overheating of the Energy Store, motor-generator systems or MGU-H can be caused by excessive use, which may lead to a loss of performance and lose valuable overtaking opportunities.
